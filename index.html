Maffetone method (MAF heart rate)

Dr Phil Maffetone’s simple ceiling for maximum aerobic effort: stay at or under this heart rate on easy/aerobic days so you train the aerobic system instead of constantly dipping into higher stress.

Suggested max aerobic HR (MAF)
145 bpm

Aerobic sweet spot: 135–145 bpm (ceiling down to −10)

Formula: 180 − age, then apply the adjustment. Keep easy runs at or under the ceiling.

  • Use a chest strap if you can — wrist HR is often late or noisy.
  • Hills: slow down or walk so HR doesn’t spike over MAF.
  • At first the pace may feel embarrassingly slow. That’s normal. Speed at that HR improves over months.
  • Quality days in this app are separate — those are where harder effort belongs.

Training Zones

Every session in your plans is run at a specific pace for a reason. Here is what each zone is for and how its pace is worked out.

Your Zone Paces

What Is Maximum Aerobic Capacity?

Running is powered by oxygen. Your maximum aerobic capacity is the most oxygen your body can take in and use in a minute while running hard. Scientists call it VO₂max.

It is measured in millilitres of oxygen, per kilogram of body weight, per minute (ml/kg/min). Dividing by body weight makes it fair to compare a small runner with a big one. A typical recreational runner sits somewhere around 35 to 50 ml/kg/min, and elite distance runners are often above 70.

Training raises it, and so does getting lighter or running more efficiently, so it is not a fixed number. A lab test is the only exact way to measure it, so this app estimates it from a recent race result instead.

How Zone Paces Are Worked Out

Your race result is converted into an estimate of your maximum aerobic capacity. Each zone is then a set percentage of that. Easy running uses roughly two thirds of it, threshold about 88%, and hard intervals close to all of it. Every pace in your plan comes from the same estimate, so the zones stay in proportion to each other.

This is why a recent, honest race result matters. If the estimate is too high, every zone is too fast. If you are unsure, train at the fitness you have now and let your times improve naturally.

The Zones And Why We Use Them

Six zones, each with a different job. Together they make a balanced plan.

Easy

About 66–70% of your maximum aerobic capacity
How it feels:
Relaxed and conversational. You can speak full sentences, and you finish feeling like you could do it again tomorrow.
Why we train here:
This is where most of your training happens. It builds your aerobic base, strengthens muscles, tendons and bones gradually, and lets you recover from harder days. It is the best protection against injury.
How the pace is set:
Worked out from a recent race result, as a percentage of your maximum aerobic capacity. The pace range runs from the fastest you should go (about 70%) down to a slower, more comfortable pace (about 66%). If in doubt, go slower.
In your plans:
All easy runs, recovery days and warm-ups. Roughly 80% of your weekly kilometres.

Long Run

Slightly slower than easy
How it feels:
Gentle and steady. The pace should feel almost too slow early on, and you should still be able to chat at the end.
Why we train here:
Builds endurance, fatigue resistance and the habit of fuelling during a run. Going a touch slower than easy keeps the extra distance from costing you more recovery.
How the pace is set:
About 62–66% of your maximum aerobic capacity, so a little slower than your easy pace.
In your plans:
Weekend long runs, and the easy parts of long runs with faster finishes or race-pace blocks. Ultra plans use this pace for every long run.

Marathon

Your marathon race pace
How it feels:
Steady and controlled. Comfortable enough to hold for hours, but you are never cruising.
Why we train here:
Teaches your body to run efficiently at race rhythm and to burn fuel well. It is also how you practise race-day fuelling, shoes and mindset.
How the pace is set:
The pace you could hold for a marathon at your current fitness, worked out from your race result.
In your plans:
Race-pace blocks inside long runs, and race-pace rehearsal sessions late in a marathon or half marathon plan.

Threshold

About 88% of your maximum aerobic capacity
How it feels:
Comfortably hard. You can say a few words at a time, and you feel like you are working but could keep going for about an hour.
Why we train here:
Raises the pace you can sustain before fatigue builds up quickly. This is the most useful quality zone for 10K to marathon runners.
How the pace is set:
About 88% of your maximum aerobic capacity, which is close to the pace you could race for roughly an hour.
In your plans:
Tempo runs, split tempos, cruise intervals and over-under sessions.

Interval

About 97% of your maximum aerobic capacity
How it feels:
Hard. Breathing is heavy, and you can only manage a word or two. Reps last about 3 to 5 minutes, with jog or rest between them.
Why we train here:
Raises your aerobic ceiling, which is the maximum amount of oxygen your body can use. This is the main speed-building zone for 5K and 10K racing.
How the pace is set:
About 97% of your maximum aerobic capacity, close to your 3K to 5K race pace.
In your plans:
800 m to mile repeats, 1 km intervals, 2 km repeats and ladder sessions.

Repetition

Around 1500 m race pace
How it feels:
Fast but relaxed and smooth. Reps are short, with long, easy recoveries, so it feels sharp rather than exhausting.
Why we train here:
Improves running economy and leg speed without much fatigue. It teaches you to run fast with good form.
How the pace is set:
About your 1500 m race pace at current fitness. Shorter reps use faster paces and longer reps use slower ones.
In your plans:
400 m and 600 m repeats, and the shorter reps in pyramids.

Why Most Of Your Running Is Easy

Hard zones work because you recover from them. The easier your easy days are, the harder and better your quality days can be. Runners who turn every run into a medium-hard effort end up too tired to do either well.

Not sure of your pace? Use the Easy Pace calculator and your zone paces will show up at the top of this page.

Paces are guidelines. Heat, hills, wind, sleep and stress all change how a pace feels, so use effort as your guide on the day.

Warm-Up & Cool-Down

A short primer before and after you run. Drawn from practical training notes — adapt what feels right for your body.

Why Warm Up?

Skipping a warm-up invites injury. A proper warm-up primes muscles, joints, ligaments, and tendons for impact, and helps heart rate rise smoothly instead of spiking hard in the first few minutes.

Static vs Dynamic Stretching

  • Before the run — dynamic only. Moving drills (leg swings, high knees, open/close gates). Avoid holding long static stretches on cold muscles.
  • Static stretching when cold can reduce power and leave joints less stable for the work ahead.
  • After the run — gentle static stretches are fine for recovery once muscles are warm.

Use before easy runs, quality sessions, and long runs. Stay relaxed — this is activation, not a workout.

1The Foundation & Gear Basics

Mindset & Strategy
  • Personal preference: Take all compiled data and training notes with a grain of salt. Everyone's physiology is unique — do your own research and trial and error to find what works for your body.
  • The ultimate goal is fun: If you're happy heading out the door and running whenever, at whatever pace, without overcomplicating things — do exactly that.
  • The golden rule: Consistently good always beats occasionally great. Stick to the plan and keep showing up.
  • Deep dive: These are personal notes from podcasts, books and general research — use them as a stepping stone for your own deeper research.
  • The gift of solitude: Carving out 30 minutes, 3 times a week, alone with your thoughts is incredibly rewarding.
Footwear Principles
  • Footwear choice: Shoes are 100% personal preference. What works for someone else might injure you.
  • Fit from day one: Shoes shouldn't need a "break-in". They should feel natural the first time you put them on.
  • Room to splay: Feet swell when you run. Choose a shoe with plenty of room for toes to spread.
  • Red flags: A proper running shoe should never give you blisters or turn your toenails black.
Data & Monitoring
  • Heart rate monitors: Wrist-based optical HR is unreliable and laggy. Invest in a quality chest strap (generally $100–$150).
  • Daily HR variables: Sleep, nutrition, hydration and mental stress all affect heart rate. Watch trends to see how lifestyle impacts running efficiency.
  • Run to time, not distance: Tracking early runs in minutes is easier to manage and scale when building an aerobic base. Save distance goals for specific workouts later.

2Training Variables & Effort Management

To run well you must use oxygen efficiently. Humans can only run anaerobically for roughly one to three minutes, so 99% of distance running is aerobic. Even elite marathoners at a 2-hour pace are aerobic — they're just extraordinarily efficient at faster paces and higher heart rates.

Understanding the Intensity Zones
  • Aerobic zone (low intensity / Zone 2): Continuous, sufficient oxygen reaches the muscles. Powers 99% of running distances from the mile to the ultramarathon. Maximises fat adaptation and triggers key cellular adaptations.
  • Anaerobic zone (high intensity): Oxygen delivery is insufficient. You can only stay here 1 to 3 minutes before exhaustion, due to rapid build-up of free-floating hydrogen ions (H⁺) in the muscles.
  • Metabolic shift: Running at a lower heart rate trains your body to burn fat as its primary fuel, preserving limited glycogen and extending your endurance ceiling.
Thresholds & Paces
  • Lactate threshold zone: The pace where your body clears lactate at the same rate it produces it. Sustainable for 40 minutes to an hour; threshold sessions typically feature 15 to 45 minutes of work.
  • Critical velocity (CV) pace: The transition zone between VO₂max and lactate threshold. Sustainable for 20 to 30 minutes and targets malleable Type 2a fibres. Roughly estimated as 85% of your all-out 1-mile effort.
  • VO₂max: The maximum oxygen your body can consume while running. An all-out effort sustainable for roughly 8 to 12 minutes.
  • Neuromuscular stimulus: Short, explosive bursts of 10 to 60 seconds (e.g. strides) that prime brain-to-muscle pathways and improve mechanics without metabolic fatigue.
Breathing & Rhythm
  • Functional breathing: Breathe however you need to get oxygen in. Nasal breathing is great daily, but breathe through your mouth while running.
  • Rhythmic footstrike breathing: Match breathing cycles to your footfalls to find flow.
EffortPattern (inhale:exhale steps)
Zone 2 / easy3:4 or 3:3
Intermediate / threshold2:3 or 2:2
VO₂max / all-out sprints2:1 or 1:1
Odd patterns (3:2, 2:3, 3:4) alternate which foot hits the ground at the start of your exhale. Impact stress is highest at the start of an exhale, so alternating balances the workload and reduces localised strain.
Recovery & Effort Checks
  • EPOC (Excess Post-Exercise Oxygen Consumption): The oxygen your body needs to recover, repair and return to resting state — paying back the oxygen "debt".
  • The breathing test: If you finish huffing, puffing and bent over at the waist, you crossed your threshold, created an oxygen deficit and went anaerobic.
  • A true aerobic finish: After a proper Zone 2 run you shouldn't feel wiped. Take a couple of deep catch-up breaths and watch HR return to normal quickly.
  • Comfort marker: Low intensity shouldn't feel gruelling. If your Zone 2 pace feels frustratingly slow, hide current pace on your watch face — show only time, heart rate and total distance.

3The Deep Science

Fuel Pathway 1: Carbohydrate
  • ATP (adenosine triphosphate) fuels every movement — muscle fibres, neurons, your heart. It's produced via two main pathways.
  • Glycolysis: A 6-carbon glucose splits instantly in the cell fluid, with no oxygen needed, into two 3-carbon pyruvate molecules. Quick yield: 2 ATP.
  • Aerobic branch (oxygen available): Pyruvate enters the mitochondria and the Krebs cycle. Total yield skyrockets to 32–36 ATP per glucose.
  • Anaerobic branch (oxygen deficient): Past your aerobic threshold, glucose splits faster than oxygen can keep up. Pyruvate backs up, free hydrogen ions explode, acidity spikes and you get the classic "muscle burn".
  • The lactate buffer: To halt the acid build-up, the body bonds hydrogen ions to pyruvate: Pyruvate + H⁺ = Lactate.
  • Recycling: Lactate is not waste — it's an energy buffer. When intensity drops, it's shipped to the liver or oxygen-rich muscle, hydrogen ions are stripped off and bound with oxygen to form harmless water (H₂O), and the clean pyruvate goes back to the mitochondria for its 32–36 ATP.
Fuel Pathway 2: Fat (strictly aerobic)
  • Fatty acid transport: Long-chain fatty acids are delivered to muscle cells from fat stores.
  • Absolute oxygen requirement: Fat can only be processed inside mitochondria, in the continuous presence of oxygen.
  • The yield: Slow and complex, but the payoff is immense: 106–130 ATP per fat molecule.
Biological Profiles & the Fuel Shift
  • Carbohydrate limits: You store only around 2,000 calories of glycogen — roughly 90 to 120 minutes of intense activity. It's the mandatory fuel for high-intensity running.
  • Lactate is not the enemy: Rising acidity from hydrogen ions causes failure, not lactate. Lactate is the helper clearing those ions, and small amounts constantly circulate and recycle through your day.
  • Fat stores: Even the leanest runners store hundreds of thousands of calories of body fat.
  • The fuel shift: The highest percentage of fat burned (relative to carbs) occurs during sleep. The absolute volume of fat burned per minute peaks in walking, light exercise and Zone 2. As intensity climbs and oxygen becomes scarce, fat burning locks out and carbs take over.

Cellular & Muscle Adaptation
  • Mitochondrial density: More mitochondria = more sustained speed at a lower physical effort.
  • Mitochondrial biogenesis: Consistent low-HR training makes your body build new mitochondria and expand existing networks. High aerobic capacity is the foundation of all running performance.
  • Mitochondrial degradation: "Use it or lose it" — density measurably declines after 7 to 10 days of complete inactivity.

Muscle fibre types

Stress, Fatigue & Overtraining
  • Central fatigue (CNS): Brain and spinal cord, from systemic physical and mental stress. Shows as brain fog, persistent lethargy and lack of mental sharpness.
  • Peripheral fatigue (muscular): Muscle cells run short of the calcium ions needed to trigger contractions. The brain commands, but the tissue physically can't execute.
  • The 3 stress buckets: Your CNS doesn't differentiate between types of stress.
  • Physical: overtraining, poor posture, wrong footwear.
  • Biochemical: processed foods, hidden food intolerances, environmental toxins.
  • Mental/Emotional: work pressure, lack of sleep, relationship anxiety.
The balance: Training must stay flexible around your life. If external stress spikes (new job, family commitments), your bucket is already full — forcing heavy volume on top makes injury, illness or burnout almost guaranteed. Knowing when to push and when to pull back is a skill often learned the hard way.

4Programming & Frameworks

General Training Principles
  • The warm-up: Skipping it invites injury. Spend 5 minutes on walking, high knees, butt-kicks, side shuffles and dynamic stretches to prime muscles, joints, ligaments and tendons and give a smooth, linear HR rise.
  • Static vs dynamic stretching: Avoid static stretching cold — it can destabilise the joint and reduce power. Save it for post-run.
  • The 80/20 rule: ~80% of weekly volume easy and conversational (Zone 2); ~20% high-intensity (Critical Velocity or Lactate Threshold).
  • Minimum effective dose: A 20-minute run gives general health benefits, but you need at least 30 continuous aerobic minutes to trigger true mitochondrial and metabolic adaptations.
  • Progression ceiling: Never increase weekly volume by more than 5–8% from the previous week.
  • Step-loading: Step volume up, hold it for 2 to 3 weeks, and only step up again once it feels completely comfortable.
  • Earn the right to go fast: Run comfortably 3–4 times a week for 30–50 minutes continuous for at least 8–12 weeks before formal speed work.

Beginner / Intermediate Reset

For runners with a base who have plateaued, are chronically fatigued, or want to rebuild their metabolic engine.

  • Phase 1 — Aerobic block: 12 weeks of at least three 30-minute runs per week at a strictly low heart rate. Apply the 5–8% progression rule if expanding distance.
  • Phase 2 — Intro to speed: After 12 weeks, slowly add light fartlek play or short relaxed strides at the end of one run per week.
  • Phase 3 — Formal progression: Move into targeted interval or speed blocks once your musculoskeletal system is fully comfortable.
Isolated Speed Workouts
  • Intro to speed (fartlek): 10 rounds of 1 min fast / 1 min easy. Brisk but controlled; you shouldn't be gasping at the end of the recovery minute.
  • Neuromuscular session: 8 × 100 m smooth accelerations on a flat straight, with a full 60 seconds of standing rest between each.
  • Critical velocity sessions: Intervals of 2–5 min (typically 800–1,500 m). For every 1,000 m run, take exactly 1 minute rest. Total speed volume 2,000–5,000 m.
  • VO₂max sessions: Intervals of no more than 3–4 min with equal-time rest (1:1) to allow hydrogen ion and lactate clearance.
  • The Norwegian 4×4: 4 reps of 4 min at VO₂max effort (roughly current 3K–5K race pace), with 4 min very easy jogging between reps.

5Race Plans

Intense intervals and speed work stress your musculoskeletal system, joints and CNS. Pushing in before your soft tissues adapt to impact is a direct path to the injury clinic.

Ground Rules
  • Prerequisite: For people who have built a strong base over months and years, not weeks.
  • The base rule: Have a large, unbroken block of Zone 2 volume banked before race-specific blocks.
  • Anti-burnout deload week: Every 3–4 weeks drop total weekly volume by 10–15%. Keep speed workouts the same but shorten easy runs.
  • Race goal realism: An aggressive goal 12 weeks out without consistent training is a recipe for disaster. Progress takes years — enjoy the compounding returns of consistency.
The 5K Plan
  • Focus: Maximise VO₂max power and neuromuscular recruitment.
  • Taper: 7 days out, drop volume by 30%. Do a light stride session (4 × 100 m) 2 days before the race.
  • Workout A — 4×4 Engine Builder: 4 × 4 min hard (est. 3K race pace) with 4 min very easy jog between.
  • Workout B — Neuromuscular Prime: 8–10 × 100 m accelerations, 1 full minute standing rest. Hit 90% top speed with smooth, upright mechanics.
  • Workout C — Speed Fartlek: 10–12 rounds of 1 min fast / 1 min easy jog.
The 10K Plan
  • Focus: CV pace and VO₂max intervals to convert Type 2a fibres into fatigue-resistant powerhouses.
  • Taper: 7 days out, drop volume by 30% and prioritise sleep to clear peripheral fatigue.
  • Workout A — 1K Cruise Blocks: 6 × 1,000 m at current 10K race pace, 60 s walk or slow jog recovery.
  • Workout C — The Hybrid: 3 × 1,500 m at CV pace (90 s rest), then immediately 4 × 100 m fast strides.

The Half Marathon Plan
  • Focus: Lactate threshold development — push your clearing pace close to your maximum aerobic ceiling.
  • Gut training: Use long weekend runs to practise fuelling: 30–60 g fast-acting carbs per hour via gels or sports drinks.
  • Taper: 14 days out cut weekly volume 25% (keep threshold intervals short). 7 days out cut by 50% to maximise glycogen storage.
  • Workout A — Continuous Threshold Block: 20–30 min unbroken at an effort you could sustain flat-out for 50–60 min.
  • Workout B — Mile Repeat Cruise: 4 × 1 mile (1,600 m) at target half-marathon pace, 2 min easy jog between.
  • Workout C — Over-Under Fartlek: 30 min continuous alternating 3 min slightly above threshold and 2 min slightly below.
The Full Marathon Plan
  • Focus: Maximise aerobic capacity (Zone 2) to optimise fat oxidation and protect your limited ~2,000-calorie glycogen store.
  • Specificity rule: At least once every two weeks, mimic your race's elevation, terrain and climate on the weekly long run.
  • Gut training: Mandatory — 30–60 g carbs per hour on all extended long runs.
  • Taper: 21 days out, cut weekly volume 25% each week; by the final week reduce by a full 50%.
  • Workout A — The Long Run: 90–150 min continuous with HR strictly capped in your aerobic zone. Walk hills if HR spikes toward threshold.
  • Workout B — Fast-Finishing Long Run: 75 min easy Zone 2, then 15–20 min right at lactate threshold pace to train clearance on tired legs.

6Pace Charts & Predictive Formulas

Thousands of calculators, charts and spreadsheets exist to estimate current fitness and project race times. These classic systems complement each other.

Maximum Aerobic Capacity & Pace Estimates
  • A recent race or all-out time trial gives a good estimate of your current maximum aerobic capacity (VO₂max).
  • That one number gives training paces for Easy, Threshold, Interval and Repetition workouts, and estimated times at other distances.
  • The progression principle: Never train at a higher level than your current fitness dictates, even with a faster goal race. Train at your current level, let it make you efficient, and your times will drop (and your aerobic capacity will rise) naturally.
Example: a maximum aerobic capacity score of 49 gives an easy running pace of roughly 5:13 per km (≈ 8:24 per mile).

The Maffetone Baseline (MAF 180)
  • Dr. Phil Maffetone's formula gives your Maximum Aerobic Heart Rate ceiling for Zone 2 base building: 180 − age.
  • Example (30-year-old runner): ceiling 150 bpm. Target zone is from the ceiling down to 10 beats below: 140 to 150 bpm.
  • Adjustment: If you've been sick, injured or inconsistent, deduct an additional 5–10 beats from the ceiling.
For a broad range of runners, the easy pace from your race result naturally aligns with the MAF 180 heart rate range. Use both as guardrails, but let your personal biology make the final decision.

7Conclusions

Final Thoughts
  • Whether your motivation is weight management, stress relief, performance goals or pure social connection, never lose sight of enjoyment.
  • Let data inform, not dictate. Numbers are valuable tools, but it's easy to become overwhelmed by metrics.
  • Comparison is the thief of joy. Avoid comparing paces or race times to other runners — you don't know their background, athletic history or lifestyle. Measure progress against your past self and consistency will take care of the rest.

Put workouts on your watch

Use your MatRob plan sessions as custom workouts on Garmin or Apple Watch. Menus can vary slightly by model and software version.

Import MatRob workouts into Garmin Connect

  1. Once you have received your training plan via email, click the download on your plan to download the ZIP. Save it to your Downloads folder, then right-click it and choose Extract All (Windows) or double-click it (Mac).
  2. Install a compatible Chrome extension that adds JSON workout importing to Garmin Connect, such as Share your Garmin Connect workout. This is third-party software, not a Garmin product.
  3. In Chrome, sign in at Garmin Connect, open Training & Planning > Workouts, then use the extension's Import Workout option to select a .json file. Repeat for each workout.
  4. Open each imported workout and select Send to Device, or schedule it in Garmin Connect. Sync your watch using the Garmin Connect app.

Garmin Connect does not provide a standard built-in import option for these JSON files. The import extension is required. Each workout name includes its week/day and, where available, its date.

Create a custom run workout (Garmin Connect app)

  1. Open the Garmin Connect app on your phone (watch must be paired).
  2. Tap More (bottom right).
  3. Tap Training & Planning → Workouts.
  4. Tap Create a Workout.
  5. Choose activity type: Run (or Cardio if Run isn’t listed).
  6. Build steps to match your MatRob session, for example:
    • Warm-up — time (e.g. 10–15 min) · easy effort
    • Run / Interval — distance or time + target pace if you want
    • Recover — jog or walk between reps
    • Use Repeat for sets like 8 × 400 m
    • Cool-down — easy time
  7. Tap Save, give it a clear name (e.g. “W3 Medium intervals”).
  8. Open that workout → Send to Device → pick your watch → sync.

You can also create workouts at connect.garmin.com on a computer, then send them to the watch.

Start it on the watch

  1. Open the activity (e.g. Run).
  2. Look for Training / Workouts / Workout Library (wording varies by model).
  3. Select your custom workout → start when ready.
  4. The watch will prompt you through each step (warm-up, intervals, cool-down).

Example: 8 × 400 m

1. Warm-up — 12 min · open / easy

2. Repeat × 8:

· Run — 400 m · target pace (optional)

· Recover — 90–120 sec walk/jog

3. Cool-down — 10 min easy

Create a custom workout on Apple Watch

  1. Open the Workout app on your Apple Watch.
  2. Turn the Digital Crown to Outdoor Run (or Indoor Run).
  3. Tap the ⋯ (More / Goals) button.
  4. Tap Custom, then Add (+).
  5. Build the session:
    • Warmup — Time or Distance (or Open)
    • Add → Work — time or distance for the hard bit
    • Add → Recovery — easy jog/walk between reps
    • Add more Work/Recovery pairs, or use repeats if available
    • Cooldown — Time or Distance
  6. Tap the title to name the workout (e.g. “Tempo 20 min”).
  7. Tap Create Workout.

On newer iOS / watchOS you can also build custom workouts in the Fitness app on iPhone (Workout tab → Goals → Custom); they sync to the watch.

Start it on the watch

  1. Open Workout → Outdoor Run.
  2. Your custom workouts appear under that activity.
  3. Select the one you made → start.
  4. The watch alerts you when each interval changes (haptics / chime).

Example: easy run + strides

Warmup — 10 min · Open or Time

Work — 20–40 min easy (match your plan distance/time)

Work — 20–30 sec stride · Recovery — 60–90 sec easy · repeat 4–6×

Cooldown — 5–10 min

Other devices & general tips

  • Coros / Polar / Suunto — similar idea: create structured workouts in their phone app or website, then sync to the watch.

  • Walk/run beginners — use Work = jog time, Recovery = walk time, and set the number of repeats to match the week’s session.
